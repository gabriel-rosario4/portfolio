## The problem

This started as a question rather than a product: **can a model I trust
disagree with a betting market often enough to matter?**

The honest answer, most of the time, is no. Markets are efficient on the
things everyone watches. Building the tool was mostly an exercise in
constructing something rigorous enough to prove myself wrong quickly, rather
than something encouraging enough to keep me betting.

## Designing against my own bias

The failure mode of a tool like this is that it flatters you. Show a model's
probability next to a market line and the eye finds "edges" everywhere — most
of them noise, all of them exciting.

Three decisions pushed back:

1. **The vig is removed before anything is displayed.** Raw implied
   probabilities sum to more than one; comparing a model against them
   manufactures edge out of the bookmaker's margin. This alone eliminated
   most apparent opportunities.
2. **Every flagged line shows its historical hit rate**, not just its current
   edge. A 3% edge on a market where the model has been wrong all season is
   not an opportunity.
3. **Results are reported after the fact, unfiltered.** The tool tracks every
   line it flagged, including the ones that lost, with no ability to hide a
   bad week.

<Callout tone="warning" title="A backtest that only ever looks good is broken">
My first backtest showed a strong return. It was selecting bets using closing
lines while claiming to have placed them at opening prices — the same
point-in-time leak I'd hit before, wearing a different hat. Once corrected, the
edge shrank to something plausible, which is to say something believable.
</Callout>

## Why DuckDB replaced Postgres

The workload is analytical, not transactional: scan every historical line for a
market, compute rolling aggregates, compare. Postgres handled it correctly and
slowly — a full-slate analysis took about four minutes, which is long enough
that I stopped running it.

Moving the analytical store to DuckDB over Parquet files took an afternoon and
cut that to six seconds:

```sql
-- Columnar storage makes this a scan of two columns, not of every row.
SELECT market_id,
       AVG(model_prob - implied_prob_novig) AS mean_edge,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE outcome = 'win') / COUNT(*)::FLOAT AS hit_rate
FROM lines
WHERE game_date >= ?
GROUP BY market_id
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 50;  -- ignore markets with too little history to judge
```

The lesson wasn't "DuckDB is fast". It was that I'd reached for the database I
knew rather than the one shaped like the problem, and paid for it in the worst
currency: a tool too slow to actually use.

## What I'd change today

**I'd separate the model from the tool.** Probabilities are computed inside the
Streamlit app, which makes them awkward to test and impossible to reuse. It
should be a library with the UI as one consumer.

**I'd track line movement, not just closing lines.** Where the line moved is
often more informative than where it landed, and I'm throwing that away.

## What I took away

The most useful thing this project produced was a habit: when a result looks
good, look for the leak before looking for the reason. Every genuinely
surprising number I've produced has, so far, been a bug — and the ones I found
quickly cost far less than the one I didn't.
